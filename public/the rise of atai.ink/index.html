Chapter 1How It All Started

Well, how do I even start? I have always been bad at starting things or conversations like this.

Writing… explaining myself… putting my thoughts into words that other people could understand—it never came naturally to me. Even when I had something important to say, I would delay it, overthink it, or avoid it completely. The way my mind worked, whenever I was about to say anything, I would try to simulate the possible outcomes of what I said, and the more negative or uncomfortable those outcomes were, the harder it became to speak. It always felt easier to stay quiet than to risk saying something the wrong way. That was just who I had been.

So even starting this book felt uncomfortable. I didn't wake up one day and suddenly feel ready to write. There was no perfect moment, no confidence, no clear plan. Just a decision that I couldn't keep everything inside anymore. And even then, I struggled. I kept asking myself, "Where do I even begin?"

Because my story isn't simple. Well, I guess no one's story is ever simple if we had the chance to read them. But for those of us who get the chance to write about our life experiences and difficulties, I hope these pages encourage you to keep thriving on your goals and aspirations, and that maybe you will write your own stories with whatever you want to tell people about. I am not writing this book to teach financial education, even though that is always my favorite topic at the moment. Most of the time, when I am on my phone or any device, I am reading about money, watching videos about business, trying to understand systems, trying to figure out how people built wealth from nothing.

That was the world I spent most of my time in. But this book isn't about that. It's about something more real, more personal, more uncomfortable—because it's about my life. My experiences of becoming a programmer in Uganda, and then building wealth as a programmer—what challenges you face and what the journey feels like. My experiences may not be your goal, but I hope this book can teach you something and help you keep moving forward, and help you understand that whatever you are facing was and is being faced by others too, so you can know that you are never alone in this world, even if you may not see it right now.

This is not the version people post online—not the clean, edited version that makes everything look possible, but the one that actually happened. The one people didn't talk about.

When I think about where everything started, my mind always goes back to when I was around ten years old. At that age, most kids were just living without thinking too much about the future—and well, so was I, for I was not special in any way. But something about us kids, or kids like me, was that we would become interested in anything shiny or rare, and for me it was phones.

I think it all came down to the fact that as I grew up, phones belonged to specific people and certain age groups. They were something no one had, which made them special to me as a kid—even seeing my seventeen-year-old brothers without a phone felt strange, because they were old, and if they did have one, they were always hiding it from my parents or the elders. That made everything about phones special—not just using them, but understanding them.

I would watch my own mom struggle to use a smartphone. The first child in our family to use one was my sister, and she got it after completing O level (what you call high school). The phone was a Tecno Pop 2, if I remember correctly, bought at ninety thousand shillings as a promotion offer. It was amazing, and it was the first phone I actually got to touch, though I never showed that much interest in phones at first. Still, owning a phone was special. I would look at a phone and wonder about the limits of its capabilities—what a phone could and could not do, how it worked, what made it so powerful. That phone wasn't just a device to me. I guess it was because it felt rare and special. It felt like something bigger, something connected to a world I didn't fully understand and yet could hardly access.

The more I thought about it, the more I wanted one. Not because everyone else had one, but because not everyone could have one. And I won't pretend my reasons were pure—I wanted it to show off and to play games on it too. Even though a phone seemed special to me, I guess I had no noble reasons for wanting one at age ten. I won't tell you the motivational nonsense that it felt like it could change my life, or that it was a key—because that was never the reason in my case. Maybe it was because I was only ten back then. But unlike most childhood interests, wanting a phone never faded. Instead, it grew stronger. And eventually, it turned into a goal when I decided that I was going to buy my own phone.

But there was one problem. No one was going to give it to me. If I wanted it, I had to work for it—which was, well, kind of impossible, since I was only ten. Yet that was where everything began. It was never easy to start, leave alone finding where to get the money to buy the phone from. So I had to save every coin I could get, and well, it wasn't much, since I was only a child. It wasn't fast at all, and it definitely wasn't something the people around me took seriously. But I kept going. I saved every small amount of money I could get. It didn't matter how little it was. I treated it like it mattered, because to me, it did.

While other people were spending, I was thinking long-term, I guess—though it never occurred to me back then. I was focused on one thing: buying my very own phone. And it took me years—not months, but well over five years. There were moments I felt like giving up, moments where it felt like it was taking too long, like it wasn't even worth it. But I kept saving and working hard, because little by little makes a difference, as they say. So by the time I reached fifteen, I had finally done it.

I had saved three hundred fifty thousand UGX.

I still remember that moment clearly, because it was surprising: the amount of money I saved in the last six months was around eight times what I had saved in the four and a half years before that. In four years and seven months, I had saved only seventy thousand Ugandan shillings—and then in the last month alone I was able to save thirty-eight thousand, before the extra charges like SIM cards and phone accessories.

It was life-changing and amazing—not just because of the money itself (well, partly because of the money itself), but because of what it represented: my time, effort, patience, and discipline, and everything I had put into something most people would have taken for granted. That was the first time I experienced the effects of compound interest indirectly.

That's how I bought my first phone, a Samsung S8, in 2024. And that moment—whether anyone else saw it or not—was the beginning of a completely different life for me. And the funny part was that I did not even notice it myself back then.

At that time, I was in Senior 2. It wasn't the first time I had ever used a phone. I had used phones belonging to my brothers and sisters before. But there is a difference between using something temporarily and actually owning it. This one was mine, and that seemed to change everything—though in actual sense it never did, because where I came from, things weren't that simple when you owned a phone earlier than you were supposed to.

In many African homes, especially the one I grew up in, there were rules that weren't written but were deeply understood. You didn't touch your father's belongings. You didn't question certain decisions. You didn't act like you had control over things. No one had to sit you down and explain it. You just knew. And if you didn't know, you learned quickly.

So owning a phone at that age wasn't normal. My parents didn't approve of it at first—and well, up to now they still don't. The only reason I was even allowed to use it was because I told them that my sister had helped me get it. That made it slightly acceptable in their eyes. But even then… they were never comfortable with it. And from that moment, something shifted in the way they saw me.

Every problem became connected to that phone. If something went wrong, it was because I had a phone. If I made a mistake, the fact that I owned a phone was the blame. If anything happened around me, somehow it came back to that one decision. It didn't matter whether it made sense. That was just how it was.

That phone wasn't just a device to me. It was where I started learning things I had never been exposed to before—like 87% of everything I know. It was where I started seeing possibilities beyond my environment, where I started imagining a different life.

And at the same time… there were things going on inside me that no one could see—or at least, I thought no one could. There were moments I sat alone, thinking about life in ways that didn't feel normal for my age. I wasn't just thinking about school, or friends, or everyday things. I was thinking deeper. Sometimes too deep for me to understand, then and even now, because there were times I questioned everything. Times I felt like life was heavier than it should have been. Times I wondered what it would feel like if everything just stopped.

Not because I wanted attention. Not because I wanted people to feel sorry for me—I worked very hard never to show anyone a reason to feel sorry for me. I believed that the weaker I looked in front of anyone, the more difficult it would be for anyone to take me seriously in this world, because people always use the messed-up moments you show them when they are trying to prove or disprove you in the future, and I never wanted to give anyone that chance over me. And yet, something like that will always happen no matter how hard you try to hide or prevent it. As long as you don't control how you react to those moments, you have not yet protected yourself from them—and such was my case, for I worked hard to prevent them but not to control how I reacted to them. Because living, sometimes, felt like something I had to endure, not something I enjoyed.

And yet, I kept going. Not because I felt strong. But because I thought about the people around me. My parents. My family, and other important people in my life. And how they would feel if I wasn't there. That responsibility stayed with me, even when everything else felt unclear.

Then there was the reality of the country I was living in. Uganda. A place where sometimes things didn't work the way they were supposed to. Where justice didn't always feel like justice. Where you could do nothing wrong and still suffer the consequences. I didn't fully understand it at first, but I experienced it—and painfully so.

There was one day I will never forget. One day that changed the way I saw everything. It was 12th May 2026, and I was seventeen years old. That day, I had gone to a place where there was free public internet. It had been provided for people to use. So I used it—and not just used it; I used it to its full capacity. There was nothing illegal about it, so we thought. And well, that was the truth.

I had just been there, using the internet. Working. Trying to build something. I had been focused. Trying to make progress on an idea that meant something to me. Something I believed could turn into something real. (Well, spoiler alert: it did turn out to be one of the most successful businesses I have built—but I'm not talking about that now.)

Then everything changed. They came without warning. Police? No—something worse: the army. And not just one or two of them, but a large group. Around 40. Some in uniform. Some not. They surrounded us. And before anyone could even understand what was happening… they started beating the people sitting there or using the public Wi-Fi, with no explanation. No warning. No questions. Just violence.

If you tried to speak, you were hit. If you tried to explain yourself, it got even worse. We were beaten. Slapped, punched, caned. Hit in ways I had never experienced before. At one point, I was hit on the head so hard that everything felt unstable. Like my body wasn't responding the way it should.

And in that moment, I kept asking myself the same question over and over again: What did I do wrong? But there was no answer. Because there was no reason.

They took us in. They locked us inside, and after beating us for some time, they made us remove our shoes. The ground was covered with sharp stones. Then they told us to run—but the exits were blocked to begin with, and not only that: the pathway that led to the exit was covered by army men and police carrying canes, blocking the gateway.

And as we tried to move, they beat us again, telling us to run and get out of their station. So in order to get out, you had to run while knocking into everything in your way, even the gate, because it was locked—and as you dodged one blow, another would land on you, and pretty hard. One landed on my head, and till now I am still surprised I am not dead from that strike. But the saddest part was that they were laughing. Like it was nothing. Like we weren't even human.

What stayed with me the most wasn't just the pain. It was the fact that people were watching. And no one said anything, even though it all happened in broad daylight. And it was treated as normal.

When I finally got out… I was injured everywhere. My body hurt. My head hurt. My feet were cut and bleeding. And the worst part was still ahead. I had to walk home. About 25 kilometers, barefoot. With those same injured feet. Every step hurt. Every movement reminded me of what had just happened. But I kept walking, because I had no other option. And even then… I couldn't show weakness.

That was the life we were living, and it was one of the many reasons why I wanted to change it. Because I believed I was caned because we were poor. If I had come from a rich or wealthy family, I would not have experienced that to begin with, and the police and the army would not have caned me, because they would have had no reason to. I believed the problem was me and no one else—the fact that I had not been able to make myself or my family rich or wealthy.

That was the environment I was trying to grow in. That was what it meant to try to become a programmer in Uganda. And yet… I didn't stop. Even after that day. Even after everything. I kept going. Because something inside me refused to give up. Not because life was easy. Not because things were working. But because stopping would mean everything I had gone through meant nothing.

So I continued learning when I could. Building when I had the chance. Failing. Trying again. And that was just the beginning.

This is not a motivational story. These are real-life experiences, and this is how it all started for me.

Chapter 2Introduction to Programming

I first heard the word "programming" when I was in Senior One, first term. At that time, I had passed ICT with 100% in that term. It wasn't something I had thought much about—it just felt normal to me. I also performed well in physics and mathematics, subjects that many people around me struggled with. But to me, it didn't feel like anything special, for I did not even try or work that hard to pass them. It was just what I could do. Yeah, really—no bragging, because I also know there are many who can do what I did and even better. That's a fact.

My elder brother was the one who noticed it first, and well, from his point of view it looked like talent, while from my point of view it was just results—nothing more or less. But that moment mattered more than I understood at the time. That day, he told me something that stayed with me. He suggested that I should become a software programmer when I grew up. That was the first time I had ever heard that word.

Programmer.

At first, it didn't mean anything to me—and that was because it came from my elder brother, the second born. Well, though he was older than us, we knew him as someone who would tell lies all the time. We believed that out of every 100 sentences he said, 84%—if not 98%—would be lies, and that was a true fact about him. Like I remember, once, during the COVID-19 lockdown in 2021… my brother, my father, and I got into a motorbike accident as we were rushing to reach home before the 7 o'clock lockdown. I won't go into much detail about the incident, but what I want to say is that while we were at the hospital, he returned home before us. And when he told the people who were at home that we had had an accident on our way back, no one believed him at all—even with the wounds he had—until he caned some of them to show them that he was serious and telling the truth. But they still refused to believe him, because he was known as a liar.

So even though it was just a word—"Programmer"… I asked him a simple question: "What is a programmer?" And his answer changed something in my mind. He told me that programmers were the people who created the apps and websites I saw and used.

At that time, I didn't even fully understand what an "app" or a "website" was, even though I used them. But I never thought about them, because I believed they just existed. And that was how life had been for me growing up in Uganda. There are things you use, see, and depend on. But you never really ask where they came from or who created them. Because deep down, we were already taught something without anyone clearly saying it—and that was that some things were not ours to create.

We grew up believing that advanced things—phones, software, machines, systems—were made by people outside of Africa. Mostly white people. That was the image. That was the assumption. That was what we were indirectly trained to believe. Not through one lesson, but through everything around us. The brands we saw, and still see. The technologies we use or used. The stories we heard. They all created a silent belief in our minds: that we as Africans were not the creators. We were the users—or at best, the fixers.

We believed that, and we still do. Because if you told at least a Ugandan that they were using something created by a fellow African or Ugandan, they would most likely hate it immediately or find issues with it. We are taught that what Africans make is not that great—and if it is great, then it was created by "black white people," or the African is just marketing it as theirs without having created it at all. That was what I also believed, and it was that belief that disturbed me even when I was getting the best title for this book. I believe we fellow Africans think we can't learn anything big from each other or from other Africans. We have real cases where people refuse to use airplanes because a fellow Ugandan is the one flying them—and it's common here.

But we also believed that maybe, if we studied hard enough, we could maybe understand these things. Maybe repair them. Maybe use them properly. But creating them? That felt like something beyond us.

So when my brother told me that there were actual people—real people—who created apps and websites… it confused me. And at the same time… it opened something in my mind. Because if someone could create these things… then it meant they don't just appear. It meant there was a process, knowledge, a way.

And then came the part that made it even more real for me. He told me that programmers were paid a lot of money. He mentioned something like 200,000 USD per month if someone worked at a big company like Google. Well, that was a lie—they earn it per year. But being who he was, even when he was trying to be truthful, he could not stop himself from adding lies to his information, no matter the reason.

At that time, I didn't fully understand how accurate that number was. But that didn't matter. What mattered was what I did next. I converted it—and as a Ugandan, money always made more sense in UGX. So I calculated it. And it came out to around 900 million UGX per month at that time.

That number didn't just surprise me. It shocked me. Because where I come from, that kind of money didn't feel real. It felt like something that only existed in movies, or in the lives of people far away from where I was. And now I was being told that someone could earn that… by creating things on a computer.

That was the moment something shifted. Not just because of the money. But because of the possibility. The idea that someone could create something—an app, a website—that people all over the world could use… that stayed in my mind. I started imagining it. What would it feel like to create something that exists on people's phones? Something they open every day? And I would be earning money from it without even working. Something that solves a problem, or becomes part of their life?

It didn't feel real, but it felt possible—because I was told so. And that was new, because for the first time, I wasn't just seeing technology as something that existed. I was starting to see it as something that could be built.

But as always, reality came in quickly. Like most people, I was told that to do programming, I needed a computer. A PC. And I needed to learn programming languages to do so—which meant finishing university. (And I know you're asking: how did I end up a Senior Four dropout, then, if I was supposed to finish university before learning programming and all of that?)

And that immediately created a barrier. Because getting a phone had already taken me years—and by then I didn't even have my phone yet, since I was told about programming in Senior One and only managed to buy the phone in Senior Two, second term. Now a computer? That felt even further away.

So I asked a simple question. One that came from curiosity, not knowledge. "Is it possible to do programming using just a phone?" But the answer I got wasn't helpful. The person I asked didn't know. They were just as new to the idea as I was at that time, because he had told me everything he knew—which meant I knew as much about programming as he did. Even my elder brother—the one who had introduced me to the idea—only knew what he had told me.

Nothing more. And that was how it started. Not with a clear path. Not with guidance. Not with resources. Just a word. A rough idea. And a possibility that I didn't fully understand yet.

Looking back now, that moment mattered more than it seemed. Because it wasn't about knowing everything. It was about being exposed to something new. Something that challenged everything I had believed before. And the fact that maybe… just maybe… I wasn't limited to being just a user.

That maybe I could become a creator, even if I had no idea how or what to do next. As Steve Jobs said, you can never connect the dots looking forward—you can only connect them looking backward. Because now, when I look back to that time, I see how it all gets connected to bring me here, even if at that time it did not. I would lose hope most of the time because it felt like I was chasing the sky, which made me stop thinking about programming altogether for long periods of my life. But that's how I was introduced to programming, in case you were wondering.

Yeah, I know it was not shiny, remarkable, or big—just something small, not even as big as I make it look. I also never took it too seriously at first, or thought it would be possible for me to do such great things in my life, or to work at a company where I would be paid two hundred thousand per month—even though now I own a company that makes that much every month, and more. But if you were to ask me back then, I would not have believed it was possible at all.

Chapter 3The First Real Encounter

When I changed schools in the second term of Senior One—and I won't say why I changed schools at that term because it's personal—everything around me changed, apart from one thing that didn't. My decision. By that time, I had already made up my mind: I was going to become a programmer, though I had forgotten it by then.

As time passed, I did not see programming like any other normal career, because well—all the other professions I had ever wanted or admired, I would forget them within the time it took to hear a more interesting and exciting one. But for some reason, the same did not happen with programming. (Yeah, I know—it still was, and had always been, the highest and best of them all in everything, from the payments to what you do or bring to the world.) That's why programming felt like something magical to me. The idea that someone could sit down and create something people could use on their phones—something real, something functional—still didn't make sense to me. But that was exactly why I wanted it. Because it didn't feel normal. It felt like a superpower.

When I finally got my phone in Senior Two, that curiosity became even stronger. Now I had a tool, even if I didn't know how to use it yet. It felt like a step forward—until, well, I forgot, and spent the next term and holiday using it to play games. My favorite games back then, the ones I spent most of my time playing, were Dream League Soccer 2024 and Call of Duty, then FIFA 24 from EA Sports—and well, watching movies and series during all of my free time. Yeah, I forgot all about programming when I received the phone, yet I claimed to be waiting for the phone so I could start learning programming. But once I got it, I immediately forgot.

It was around that time that I met someone who changed the direction of my journey, though it never looked like it at first, when we met at my new school—the school I had moved to.

Note: For reference, the school I studied at in Senior One first term was Isahaka Adventist College. When I changed schools, I changed to Bright Academy Senior Secondary School, Kasese—that's where I studied from Senior One second term to Senior Two third term. And in Senior Three, I changed to Kasese Secondary School, where I studied from Senior Three to Senior Four.

His name was Mubarak. Well, yeah—I only knew his first name, or was it his second name? I don't remember. But to me, that alone was an achievement, because I never liked knowing people's names. I identified people through facts they said or expressions I received from them—like there was someone I called "the guy who wants to be a trillionaire of Kasese"—so knowing people's names was never in my genes.

Mubarak was the first person I met in real life who actually knew what programming was. Not just the word. Not just the idea. But the actual thing. And not only that—he knew web development. HTML. CSS. JavaScript.

At that time, those words sounded complicated, almost unreal. But to him, they were normal, and it was amazing how he could even remember all the names of the languages. He would name around 12 languages straight from his head. I remember watching him and feeling something I hadn't felt before. It wasn't just curiosity anymore. It was admiration. Because for the first time, I wasn't hearing about programming from someone who had "read it somewhere." I was seeing someone who was actually doing it—even though it was just HTML, CSS, and JavaScript only, which were the basics of web development. But to me at that time, it was magical. And that changed everything.

So I did something that wasn't natural for me. I tried to become his friend. That might sound simple to you, but for me, it wasn't—because I never liked making friends or having people who knew my business or me at all. I liked being different, not understood, and while no one had any idea about me or anything related to me beyond the obvious—in other words, I wasn't the kind of person who liked making friends. I preferred being alone: thinking, planning, talking to myself, and working on my own ideas without distractions.

But this time was different. Because I knew that if I stayed in my comfort zone, I would never learn anything about programming. So I forced myself.

At school, I would sleep through lessons—and that was because I was used to working in carpentry after the school programs were over. That's how I was able to make the money I needed to buy my phone, so working after school became a habit of mine even after I had bought the phone. Whenever I reached school, I would sleep the entire day, through almost every lesson. Not because I didn't care—but because my mind was tired the entire time and focused on something else.

Meanwhile, Mubarak would do the opposite. He would dodge classes. Sneak away. And go straight to the computer lab.

The computer lab had about 12 working desktops. Old machines with 2GB of RAM and Intel Core 2 processors. I remember those details clearly, because I used to read the specifications every time I got the chance. Even when I didn't fully understand what they meant, I still paid attention. And on those computers… he built websites. Not big ones. Not perfect ones. But real ones. Well, not actual interactive websites—they were simple webpages. But to me then, they were websites, and it was amazing, fully special, how capable he was to build things that actually worked. Things you could see. Things you could interact with—all using code, using programming languages. And every time I saw that, it did something to me. It made everything feel more real. I wanted that.

So I started asking questions—a lot of them. Like: What is this code? Why is it written like that? How does it work? How can I learn this? How can I build something like that? But the answers I got were always the same. "Even if I told you, you wouldn't understand—because these are not things anyone can learn or do."

At first… I didn't take it badly. In fact, it made it feel even more special, because if programming or coding was so complex that I couldn't understand it even if they explained it to me… then it must be important and not competitive—meaning if I learned it, I would be among the special few who could program in the world. And it was special at the time.

But as time went on… that feeling changed. It stopped feeling like mystery and started feeling like rejection. And I hated being rejected with all my life and mind—of all the things someone could do to me, the worst was to reject me. And that's what he was doing, or seemed to be doing—because I wasn't being guided. I wasn't being taught. I was being shut out. Every time I asked, I got the same response: "You wouldn't understand."

And slowly… that started to disturb me. Not because he was wrong, but because I wanted to understand—or at least be told something real, anything about what he was doing or what he wrote in Notepad, because I wanted somewhere to start from. That was all I wanted. I never wanted him to teach me or be my teacher. I wanted him to guide me on where to start or how to start learning programming—and that was all I wanted. Because to me, even just a little guidance would have made a difference. But he never provided it, and never seemed to in the future either.

So instead of waiting for someone—or him—to teach me… I decided to try on my own. Because as I had learned from Mark Cuban, you don't become the guy by being the guy who knows the guy. In order to be the guy, you become the guy whom the guy needs help from—not the guy who knows the guy. You may not understand it now, but that's okay, because it took me years to finally understand it, so…

During the holidays of second term, I made a decision. I was going to learn programming, so that I could prove to him that I could understand everything he wrote in Notepad if he gave me the chance.… Come to think of it, I wanted to prove to him that I was more capable than he thought, so… no matter how little I understood or knew at the time.

I didn't know where to start. So I did what felt logical at the time. I went to the Play Store. And I searched for anything related to programming. Every language I had heard of… HTML. CSS. JavaScript. Anything with the word "developer." Anything with the word "coding." And I installed all of them.

My phone became full of apps I didn't understand. But I didn't care. I believed that somewhere inside those apps… there was something I needed to learn.

So I started reading. One app at a time. One topic at a time. Every free moment I had during that holiday… I used it to try and understand something. Anything. But nothing was working, because… no matter how much I read… no matter how many apps I opened… I wasn't understanding anything. The words didn't make sense. The code looked confusing. Nothing stayed in my head. I couldn't remember anything. I couldn't apply anything.

And slowly… it started to feel like I was wasting my time. That was one of the first times I felt close to giving up. Because I had tried. I had actually tried, to the point that my head would pain from frustration. And it still wasn't working.

So I made another decision. I deleted everything. All the apps. All the tools. Everything I had installed. I went back to zero—because I was now giving up. And for a moment… it felt like that was the end of it. Until something unexpected happened.

One day, I bought a Lyca Mobile SIM card. Because I had given up on programming, I was now projecting all my time and effort into playing video games on my phone—and because of that, I had fallen way behind my brothers, whom I used to play against in DLS, Call of Duty, or even FIFA. Because now their teams and players were stronger and more equipped than mine, since I had spent all my free time "learning programming," which had backfired and failed completely. I didn't even seem to understand or learn anything from all the apps I had installed, like I was taught valuable lessons which I failed to even apply or use.

The fact was that I would learn for hours, and when I tried to write code in my code editor on the web—which was CodePen—I would come to the understanding that everything I had learned was all nothing, because I would go blank the moment I opened CodePen to write or practice what I had read. And it kept happening for weeks, until I decided to give up and start spending my time like everyone else in my family—that was, through playing games and bragging about our teams' and players' capabilities.

But playing games needed an internet connection, which was expensive to buy on my MTN and Airtel SIM cards.
