# Growth & marketing playbook

Building the product is half the work — getting customers is the other half. Atai includes a set of growth tools so you can research your market, create content, run ads, check your search visibility, and learn what to do next — all without being a marketer.

This playbook explains each tool in plain language, what to do with it, and what to do when something isn't available yet. You don't need marketing experience to follow it. Read the section you need, try one thing, then come back.

> **New to Atai?** Start with the [Getting Started guide](/docs/onboarding), then see [pricing](/pricing) and the [resource library](/resources).

## 1. The one idea behind all of this

Most small businesses don't fail at growth because they lack tools. They fail because they try everything at once, get no results in two weeks, and stop.

So this playbook is built around one idea: **pick one audience, one promise, and one channel — and repeat until it works.**

Everything below serves that. The research tools tell you which audience and promise. Content Studio and Ads give you the channel. The SEO audit and the overview tell you what to fix next. None of it is magic, and none of it needs to be done in a single week.

If you only remember one thing: **consistency beats intensity.** Three small actions a week for three months will beat one frantic launch weekend every time.

## 2. Where the growth tools live

Open your project first. The growth tools appear in your project's menu (**Growth**, **Studio**, **SEO**, **Ads**, **Campaigns**). They work on that specific project, so always start by opening the project you want to grow.

If you're not sure where to begin, open the **growth overview** — it points you to the right next step.

Two things worth knowing before you start:

- **Growth tools work on published projects.** Some of them (especially the SEO audit) need a live site to look at, because there's nothing to check until your project is published.
- **Tools improve as your project gets more detailed.** The AI behind content and campaigns reads your business plan. A thin plan produces thin output. This is the single biggest reason people get disappointing results — see section 5.

## 3. Growth overview

Your growth overview is the starting point, and it's the page to open when you feel stuck. It brings together:

- **Market research** — who your customers are and what they want.
- **Competitors** — what others offer and where you can stand out.
- **Launch status** — how ready your business is to attract customers.
- **Recommended next steps** — a short, prioritized list.

The "recommended next steps" list is deliberately short. It's not trying to show you everything; it's trying to show you the *next* thing. Work down the list, one item at a time, and re-open the overview after each one — the list changes as you make progress.

You can also ask the AI co-founder "what should I do to grow?" and it will use the same information. That's often faster than clicking around.

### How to read your launch status

Launch status is a rough readiness signal, not a grade. It typically reflects things like:

- Does the project have a clear description of what it sells and who it's for?
- Is the site published and reachable?
- Are the basics in place — a way for people to contact you, buy, or sign up?
- Is there any content that explains the offer?

If it's low, don't panic. Open the next-steps list and fix the top item. Most of the time the top item is something small and unglamorous, like "write a clearer description of your offer."

## 4. Market research

Before you write a single post or spend a single shilling on ads, you need to know who you're talking to. Market research in Atai helps you answer that.

### The four questions that matter

Almost every useful piece of marketing answers four questions:

1. **Who exactly is this for?** Not "small businesses" — "a two-person salon in Kampala that takes bookings over WhatsApp."
2. **What problem do they have?** In their words, not yours.
3. **What do they do about it today?** Spreadsheets, a notebook, a competitor, nothing at all.
4. **Why would they switch to you?** Cheaper, faster, easier, safer, or simply available.

If you can answer those four in plain sentences, you can write good marketing. If you can't, no tool will save you — go back and answer them.

### Using the research tools

The research area of your project helps you build this picture. Treat its output as a **draft to argue with**, not a verdict. It's very good at giving you language and angles you hadn't considered; it's not a substitute for talking to five real customers.

A practical loop:

1. Run the research with a specific description of your business.
2. Read the output and highlight the phrases that sound like something a customer would actually say.
3. Write down three sentences you could imagine a customer saying about their problem.
4. Save those — they become your headlines later.

### A cheap reality check

Before trusting any research output, do this: message five people who match your target customer and ask one question — *"How do you handle [the problem] today?"* Don't pitch. Just listen. Ten minutes of real answers beats ten pages of generated research.

## 5. Competitor research

Competitors tell you two useful things: what customers already expect, and what nobody is doing yet.

### What to look for

When you look at a competitor, note:

| Look at | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **What they charge** | Sets the range customers expect. You can go lower, but you must explain why. |
| **What they promise first** | The headline on their homepage is what they think sells. |
| **What customers complain about** | Reviews and comments are free market research. |
| **How you'd describe them in one line** | If you can't, their positioning is weak — that's an opening. |
| **What they don't do** | The gap you can own. |

### Where the opening usually is

For a small business, the realistic advantages are almost never "more features." They're:

- **Narrower focus.** You serve one kind of customer really well; a big competitor serves everyone adequately.
- **Faster response.** You reply in minutes; they reply in days.
- **Local knowledge.** You understand the market, the language, the payment habits.
- **Simpler product.** Fewer buttons, less to learn.
- **Honest pricing.** No surprise fees.

Pick one of those and lean on it everywhere — your homepage, your ads, your posts. Being "the simplest option for X" beats being "a bit better at everything."

### Don't copy — position

Copying a competitor's homepage gets you their weaknesses without their strengths. Instead, use their page as a checklist of what customers expect to see, then write your own version that's clearer and more specific.

## 6. Finding your angle

Your angle is the one sentence that makes someone choose you. It has a shape:

> **For** [specific customer] **who** [has this problem], **we** [do this one thing] **so they can** [get this result] — **unlike** [the alternative].

Example: *For a two-person salon that takes bookings over WhatsApp, we give you a booking page and reminders so you stop losing appointments — unlike a notebook, which forgets.*

You don't have to publish that sentence word for word. But if you can't fill in the blanks, your marketing will wander. Write it down once, then reuse the pieces.

### Three tests for a good angle

- **The "so what" test.** Read your headline. Would a stranger care? If the answer is "that's nice," rewrite it around a result, not a feature.
- **The "who else" test.** Could a competitor say the exact same sentence? If yes, it isn't an angle yet.
- **The "prove it" test.** Can you back the claim up with something visible — a screenshot, a number, a customer quote? If not, soften it.

## 7. Content Studio

Content Studio is where you create the content that brings people in. It has four parts.

### Brand

Set a consistent voice and look — your name, tone, colours, and style. When everything sounds and looks the same, people remember you.

Start with a short description of who you are and who you serve. Something like: *"We help small salons take bookings without phone calls. Friendly, plain-spoken, no jargon."* That single paragraph is what keeps generated content on-brand — vague brand notes produce generic content.

Worth deciding early:

- **Tone:** formal or casual? Short sentences or longer? Do you use slang?
- **Words you use:** the words your customers use (say "booking", not "reservation", if that's what they say).
- **Words you avoid:** jargon, hype, anything that sounds like a brochure.
- **Visuals:** one or two colours and a font, used consistently.

### Calendar

Plan and schedule posts so you're not scrambling for ideas. A simple rhythm — a few posts a week — beats a burst of activity followed by silence.

A workable starting calendar:

| Day | What to post | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Monday | One useful tip related to your offer | Builds trust without selling |
| Wednesday | One thing about how your product works | Explains the value |
| Friday | One proof point — a result, a review, a before/after | Builds confidence |

That's three posts a week. It's enough. You can add more once it's a habit.

When you plan, work in **themes** rather than individual posts. "This month is about saving time" gives you ten post ideas instantly; "I need ten posts" gives you a blank page.

### Library

Keep reusable copy and assets: headlines, descriptions, images, and templates. Reusing good content saves time and keeps your message consistent.

Things worth saving in the library:

- Your one-line description (used on the homepage, in ads, in your email signature).
- Three headlines that worked.
- A short "about us" paragraph at two lengths — 50 words and 150 words.
- Your best three customer quotes.
- Image templates so new graphics look like the old ones.

### Campaigns / Promote

Turn content into a coordinated push. Group related posts and messages around a goal — a launch, a sale, a new feature — and promote them together.

A campaign is not "post more." It's several pieces pointing at the same outcome, so a person who sees one and then another understands the same message twice. That repetition is what makes marketing work.

A simple routine: draft in Studio, then promote your best pieces. Don't promote everything — promote the pieces that already got a reaction.

## 8. Writing content that actually works

You don't need to be a writer. You need to be clear. These rules do most of the work.

### Lead with the result, not the feature

- Weak: "Our app has automated SMS reminders."
- Strong: "Stop losing bookings because someone forgot."

The feature can come second. The result comes first.

### Be specific

- Weak: "Save time on admin."
- Strong: "Cut booking admin from 20 minutes a day to 2."

Specific numbers are believable. Round claims aren't.

### Write one idea per piece

A post that says three things says nothing. Pick one idea, say it well, stop. Shorter than you think is usually right.

### Use the customer's words

If customers say "I keep double-booking myself," use that phrase. It's more persuasive than "calendar conflict management," because it sounds like their life.

### Always give one next step

Every piece should end with a single, obvious action: "Try it free", "See how it works", "Reply and I'll send the guide." Two calls to action means neither gets clicked.

### Match the format to the place

- **Short social post:** one idea, one hook, one link.
- **Email:** a subject line that promises something specific, a short body, one link.
- **Landing page:** headline, proof, explanation, call to action — in that order.
- **Ad:** who it's for, what it does, what to do next — in under 15 words if you can.

### A quick self-check before publishing

1. Would I stop scrolling for this?
2. Is it about the customer, or about me?
3. Is there exactly one thing to do next?
4. Could a competitor post the identical thing? (If yes, sharpen it.)
5. Is every claim true and provable?

## 9. SEO audit

The SEO audit checks your published site for search-engine readiness and gives you a prioritized list of fixes. "SEO" just means: can people find you when they search for what you do?

### What it looks at

- **Titles and descriptions** — what people see in search results.
- **Structure** — clear headings and a sensible page layout.
- **Speed** — how quickly pages load.
- **Readiness** — whether the site is set up to be found.

### How to use it

1. Publish your project first — there's nothing to audit until the site is live.
2. Run the audit.
3. Work through the list from the top — the biggest wins are usually first.
4. Publish your changes and run the audit again.

Run it after you publish, and again after big changes. Think of it as a health check, not a one-off task.

### Reading the results without panic

An audit will almost always show *something*. That's normal — even large, well-run sites have items on the list. Don't try to fix everything.

Prioritise in this order:

1. **Anything that stops pages being found at all** (blocked pages, broken structure).
2. **Titles and descriptions** — they're what people click on in search results.
3. **Speed** — large images are the usual culprit.
4. **Everything else.**

If a fix takes more than an hour and isn't in the first two groups, come back to it later.

## 10. SEO basics you can do yourself

The audit finds problems; these habits stop them happening.

### Say what you do, in words people search

Your homepage title should contain the thing you sell and the place or audience you serve. "Bookings for salons in Kampala" is searchable. "Reimagining appointment experiences" is not.

### One page, one topic

Each page should answer one question. A page trying to cover pricing, features, and your life story ranks for none of them.

### Use real headings

Headings aren't decoration — they tell search engines and screen readers what a page is about. One main heading per page, then subheadings in order.

### Write for people, then check for search

Write the page as if explaining to a customer. Then make sure the words they'd search for appear naturally — in the title, the first paragraph, and a heading or two. Don't stuff keywords; it reads badly and doesn't help.

### Name your images

"booking-page-screenshot.png" is better than "IMG_2841.png", and the description field (alt text) should say what the image shows.

### Keep pages fast

Big images are the most common cause of slow pages. Compress images before uploading, and don't autoplay heavy video on the homepage.

### Be findable off your own site

Search engines also look at who links to you. A listing in a local directory, a mention in a community group, a partner's website — each one helps. This is slow, but it compounds.

## 11. Ads & campaigns

### Ads

Create and manage ad creatives and where they appear. Keep your message simple and specific: who it's for, what it does, and what to do next.

A good ad reads like a helpful sentence, not a billboard slogan:

> "Salon owners: take bookings online, send automatic reminders. Free to try."

That's it. Audience, benefit, action. If your ad needs a paragraph to make sense, it isn't ready.

### Campaigns

Group several ads into one coordinated push with a schedule and a goal. A campaign helps you measure results across several pieces at once instead of guessing from a single ad.

Give every campaign one goal, written as a number: "20 signups", "50 bookings", "100 visits to the pricing page". A campaign without a number can't succeed or fail — it just runs.

### A sensible approach

1. **Start small.** One campaign, one audience, one message.
2. **Measure.** See what actually got clicks and signups.
3. **Scale what works.** Put more behind the message that performed.
4. **Stop what doesn't.** Don't keep spending on something that isn't working.

### What to change when ads don't work

Change **one** thing at a time, or you won't know what fixed it. In rough order of impact:

1. **The audience.** Wrong people see a perfect ad and do nothing.
2. **The promise.** Right people, wrong benefit, same result.
3. **The first line.** Most people read one line and decide.
4. **The landing page.** A great ad pointing at a confusing page wastes the click.
5. **The image.** Matters, but less than the four above.

### A word on spending

Only spend money you're willing to lose while you learn. The first campaign is a test, not an investment. Decide in advance how much a customer is worth to you, and don't spend more than that to acquire one.

## 12. Landing pages and conversion

Traffic is useless if the page it lands on doesn't convince anyone. "Conversion" just means: did the visitor do the thing you wanted?

### The five-part landing page

1. **Headline** — the result, for a specific person.
2. **Proof** — a number, a review, a screenshot, a logo.
3. **Explanation** — how it works, in three short steps.
4. **Objection handling** — price, effort, risk. Answer them before they're asked.
5. **One call to action** — repeated, not varied.

### The most common landing page mistakes

- **No clear next step.** The visitor has to hunt for what to click.
- **Talking about the company instead of the customer.** Nobody cares how long you've been in business until they know what you'll do for them.
- **Too many choices.** Every extra button splits attention.
- **No proof.** Claims without evidence are just opinions.
- **Asking for too much too early.** Every field in a form costs you signups. Ask for the minimum.

### Test one thing at a time

Change the headline, wait for enough visitors to judge, then decide. Changing five things at once tells you nothing except "something changed."

## 13. Email and keeping customers

Getting a customer is expensive. Keeping one is cheap. Most growth comes from the second sale, not the first.

### The three emails every business needs

- **Welcome.** Sent immediately after signup. Says what to do first, in one step.
- **Follow-up.** Sent a few days later. Answers the most common question and offers help.
- **Reactivation.** Sent to people who signed up but never came back. One short nudge, no guilt.

You don't need a complicated sequence. These three, written plainly, do most of the work.

### Rules that keep you out of the spam folder

- Only email people who asked to hear from you.
- Make it easy to unsubscribe, and honour it immediately.
- Don't buy lists. Ever.
- Keep the subject line honest — a promise the email doesn't keep gets you marked as spam.

### Ask for reviews

A happy customer is your best marketing, and most will say yes if you ask at the right moment — right after they got the result, not weeks later. Make it one click, and thank them.

## 14. Referrals

Referrals give you a link to share. When someone signs up through it, you earn free credits — the easiest way to extend your balance without paying.

- Share your link with people who genuinely need what you're building.
- Make sure the person uses your exact link and verifies their email.
- Credits are granted after the new person verifies their email.

### How to ask without feeling awkward

The best referral ask is specific and timed:

- **After a win:** "Glad that worked — if you know another salon with the same problem, here's my link."
- **In your product:** a small, dismissible prompt after a successful action.
- **In your signature:** one line, no pressure.

Don't spam your link into unrelated groups. It burns goodwill and gets you removed.

## 16. Measuring: the numbers that matter

You can't improve what you don't look at, but most dashboards are noise. Track a handful of numbers.

| Number | What it tells you |
|---|---|
| **Visitors** | Are people finding you? |
| **Signups (or enquiries)** | Does the page convince them? |
| **Activation** | Do they do the one thing that makes the product useful? |
| **Repeat use** | Do they come back? |
| **Cost per customer** | Are you spending less than a customer is worth? |

Five numbers is enough. Look at them weekly, not hourly — daily numbers bounce around and teach you nothing.

### A simple weekly check

Write down the five numbers every Friday. After four weeks you'll see a trend. Trends are actionable; single days aren't.

If a number is flat, ask one question: *is this a traffic problem or a conversion problem?* Flat visitors means nobody's finding you — work on content and SEO. Plenty of visitors but no signups means the page or offer is wrong.

## 17. A simple weekly growth routine

You don't need to do everything at once. A steady rhythm beats a burst of activity.

- **Monday:** open the growth overview and pick one next step.
- **Tuesday:** create or refresh one piece of content in Studio.
- **Wednesday:** share or promote that content.
- **Thursday:** check what got attention and note what worked.
- **Friday:** make one small improvement to your site or offer.

Repeat. Small, consistent effort compounds.

If a week goes badly, do the Monday and Wednesday items only. Keeping the habit alive matters more than the volume.

## 18. A 90-day plan

If you want a sequence rather than a routine, this is a reasonable one.

### Days 1–30: get the basics right

- Write your one-line description and your angle.
- Publish the site and run the SEO audit once.
- Fix the top three audit items.
- Set up the three emails (welcome, follow-up, reactivation).
- Post three times a week, every week.

**Goal:** a site that explains itself and a habit that exists.

### Days 31–60: find what resonates

- Run one small ad campaign with one audience and one promise.
- Try three different headlines on the same offer.
- Talk to five customers and write down their exact words.
- Double down on whichever post format got the most response.

**Goal:** evidence about which message works.

### Days 61–90: do more of what works

- Put more budget behind the best-performing campaign.
- Turn your best post into a landing page.
- Ask your five happiest customers for a review or referral.
- Fix the next three audit items.

**Goal:** a repeatable channel, not a one-off spike.

## 19. Common mistakes

These come up constantly, and each one is fixable.

- **Trying every channel at once.** Pick one, do it for a month, then judge.
- **Marketing before the offer is clear.** If you can't explain it in a sentence, no ad will fix it.
- **Changing everything after one bad week.** Results lag. Give a change enough time to be measurable.
- **Promoting features instead of outcomes.** People buy the result.
- **Ignoring existing customers.** The cheapest growth is usually the second sale.
- **Publishing inconsistently.** Three posts a week for a year beats thirty in one week.
- **Spending on ads before the landing page works.** Fix the page first; it's free.
- **Never asking for the sale.** End every piece with one clear next step.
- **Copying a competitor's message.** You inherit their weaknesses.
- **Judging by likes instead of signups.** Attention isn't the goal; customers are.

## 20. Why a tool might be unavailable

Some growth tools are gradually rolled out, or can be turned on or off in **Settings**. If a tool shows "Coming soon" or appears greyed out, it may not be available in your workspace yet, or it may need to be enabled in Settings.

This is normal and not a sign that something is broken. Features are released gradually so they can be tested properly. If you can't turn it on, it simply isn't available for your account yet — check back later or ask support.

Meanwhile, most of this playbook works without any special tool:

- Research: talk to five customers.
- Content: write three posts a week in a notes app.
- SEO: check your page titles by hand.
- Email: send a plain welcome message.
- Referrals: ask people directly.

## 21. Common problems

### A growth tool is greyed out or says "Coming soon"
- *Cause:* it may not be enabled for your workspace yet, or it's still rolling out.
- *Fix:* check Settings for a toggle. If you can't enable it, it isn't available for your account yet — check back later or contact support.

### The SEO audit won't run
- *Cause:* your project isn't published yet, so there's no live site to check.
- *Fix:* publish your project first, then run the audit again.

### Content Studio or campaigns give weak results
- *Cause:* your project description or plan is thin, so the AI has little to work with.
- *Fix:* improve your plan first — a clear offer, audience, and pricing — then generate content again.

### The content doesn't sound like me
- *Cause:* the brand section is empty or too vague.
- *Fix:* write two or three sentences about your tone and the words you use, then regenerate. Edit the first draft rather than regenerating endlessly — a few small edits usually gets it close.

### My referral credits didn't arrive
- *Cause:* the person didn't use your link, or hasn't verified their email yet.
- *Fix:* make sure they signed up with your exact link and verified their email. Referral credits are granted after verification.

### My ads aren't getting results
- *Cause:* the message is too broad, the audience is wrong, or the goal is unclear.
- *Fix:* narrow your audience, make one clear promise, and give people one obvious next step. Start with one small campaign before scaling.

### People visit but nobody signs up
- *Cause:* usually the headline, the proof, or the number of steps to sign up.
- *Fix:* rewrite the headline around a result, add one piece of visible proof, and remove any unnecessary form fields.

### I don't know what to post
- *Cause:* no theme, so every post starts from a blank page.
- *Fix:* pick a monthly theme and list ten questions customers ask you. Each question is a post.

### My calendar is full but nothing gets published
- *Cause:* planning has become a substitute for publishing.
- *Fix:* cut the plan to three posts a week and publish the first one today. A small published plan beats a large unread one.
